# New winners, new Olympic places—and the access questions behind them

As of: 2026-10-14

India’s women’s recurve team and Indonesia’s women’s compound champion offer two striking reasons to follow competitive archery this month. Their victories expand the picture of who is winning. Separate Olympic quota allocations also clarify an emerging competition opportunity. For women preparing to move beyond club shooting, however, these developments raise another question: what resources make participation possible before medals enter the picture?

Ankita Bhakat, Kirti Sharma and Kumkum Mohod beat Korea 5–3 to secure India’s first Asian Games women’s team recurve gold, according to the Indian Express. <!-- 01-c1 --> Its account describes a final at 70 metres in which the opening two sets were tied. India won the third, then secured the decisive fourth-set tie after an arrow’s score was revised upward. That progression explains how the match was won; the final score alone cannot establish why these athletes succeeded. [Indian Express, 2 October](https://indianexpress.com/article/sports/ankita-kirti-kumkum-india-recurve-archery-gold-asian-games-korea-dominance-end-10903572/)

In the separate women’s compound individual event, Nurisa Dian Ashrifah defeated Amaya Amparo Cojuangco of the Philippines 139–137. World Archery reports that this was Indonesia’s first Asian Games archery gold. <!-- 01-c2 --> The archers were level after four ends; Ashrifah’s final three arrows supplied the winning margin. This was an individual compound result, distinct from India’s team recurve victory. The supplied compound report does not specify shooting distance, so it cannot support a distance comparison between the events. [World Archery, 1 October](https://www.worldarchery.sport/news/202661/20-year-old-ashrifah-wins-indonesias-first-asian-games-gold-jongho-delivers-korea)

The Olympic development needs equally careful reading. World Archery identifies India’s Chikitha Taniparthi and Sahil Rajesh Jadhav as the first compound mixed team to secure LA28 quota places, ahead of that category’s Olympic debut. <!-- 01-c3 --> The opportunity described is compound **mixed team**, not an Olympic women’s compound individual event. Ashrifah’s individual title and India’s mixed-team qualification therefore belong to different competitive stories. [World Archery, 6 October](https://www.worldarchery.sport/news/202662/first-angeles-2028-olympic-quotas-awarded-asian-games)

The same report’s national quota tracker lists three places for India: one compound men’s place, one compound women’s place and one recurve women’s place. <!-- 01-c4 --> These are national allocations. They do not establish which individual athletes will ultimately be selected. For an aspiring competitor reading Olympic headlines, the practical distinction is between a country obtaining a place and an athlete obtaining selection. This evidence establishes the former. [World Archery quota tracker](https://www.worldarchery.sport/news/202662/first-angeles-2028-olympic-quotas-awarded-asian-games)

Achievement also deserves to be read alongside the reported conditions of entry. The Times of India says Bhakat used wooden and bamboo bows for six years before obtaining recurve equipment, and that left-handed equipment was particularly difficult to obtain. <!-- 01-c5 --> This is a newspaper’s account of one athlete’s equipment history, rather than a survey of availability or an audited record of costs. It nevertheless identifies a concrete issue that a medal table cannot reveal: access to equipment suited to the person shooting. [The Times of India, 2 October](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/sports/asian-games-2026/arrows-that-rewrote-history-how-ankita-kirti-and-kumkum-gave-their-fathers-faith-a-golden-finish-at-asian-games/articleshow/134643396.cms)

Read together, the Indian Express match report and that equipment account support a bounded interpretation: a winning team can include someone who previously faced substantial equipment barriers. <!-- 01-c6 --> World Archery’s results and quota reporting add the competitive destinations; the newspaper histories invite scrutiny of the resources needed to reach them. This connection makes access part of the achievement story. It does not demonstrate that barriers are widespread, that they caused particular results, or that they are now easing. [Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/sports/ankita-kirti-kumkum-india-recurve-archery-gold-asian-games-korea-dominance-end-10903572/); [The Times of India](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/sports/asian-games-2026/arrows-that-rewrote-history-how-ankita-kirti-and-kumkum-gave-their-fathers-faith-a-golden-finish-at-asian-games/articleshow/134643396.cms)

There are substantive limits to this picture. Three publishers contribute, but separate mastheads do not prove independent sourcing of overlapping final details or family histories. World Archery can establish its published results and allocations while also having an institutional interest in promoting the sport. Its two reports share one producer and announcement chain; they are not two independent confirmations. The equipment histories lack financial records and accounts from women whose participation ended before elite success.

For club women, these reports suggest useful questions to take into competition planning: which discipline and event format is the intended destination, what equipment is accessible, and what remains uncertain about progression? They supply no para-specific evidence and cannot establish para eligibility or pathways. Amateur women, less-resourced countries and non-English voices also remain insufficiently represented. The next meaningful access story would examine those experiences directly, rather than treating new champions as proof of easier entry.
